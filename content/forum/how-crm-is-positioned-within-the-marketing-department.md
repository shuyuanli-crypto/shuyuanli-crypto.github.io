Walk into ten different marketing departments and ask where CRM sits on the org chart, and you'll get ten different answers. Buried under "digital." Folded into "loyalty." Treated as a channel next to email and SMS, reporting into whoever owns the newsletter calendar. Or, in the rare best case, given its own seat at the table next to brand and communications. That positioning isn't a technicality: it decides how much budget CRM gets, how much authority it has to push back when a campaign plan ignores the client relationship, and ultimately how seriously the rest of the organization takes it.

I've seen this play out differently at every company I've worked with, and the differences were never really about company size. They were about what marketing leadership believed CRM *was*.

## Where CRM usually ends up

At the research end of my career, at **IPSOS (BVA)**, I saw this from the outside: brands like L'Oréal, Chanel, Richemont, Tiffany, and Estée Lauder each had CRM sitting in a slightly different place, and it showed in what they asked us to measure. Some wanted us to track a single channel's open rates. Others wanted us to understand whether a client felt recognized across the whole relationship. The brief told you exactly how CRM was positioned internally, before anyone said a word about org charts.

At **Chanel**, working at the European level, CRM sat squarely inside marketing execution: a channel discipline with real rigor (segmentation, A/B testing, a genuine craft to it), but still reporting up through the marketing plan rather than shaping it. At **PUIG (Byredo)**, rolling out a global Clienteling ecosystem, the positioning shifted: CRM became the connective layer between marketing and retail, which meant it had to answer to both, and that dual accountability actually gave it more leverage, not less. At **ICICLE**, where I owned CRM end to end (strategy, database, budget, and the retail ambassadors), it wasn't positioned as a function at all. It was closer to a second P&L: judged on the revenue it generated, not on how well it executed someone else's plan.

## Why the positioning matters more than the org chart suggests

The common failure mode is treating CRM as a *channel*, one more line item alongside social, paid media, and content, instead of as the layer that should be informing all of those. A channel gets a budget and a calendar. A strategic function gets a seat when the marketing plan is still being written, before the campaigns are locked. The difference shows up in small but telling ways: whether CRM is consulted on a launch timeline or just told when to send the email about it; whether client data feeds back into segmentation decisions or just gets reported on after the fact.

Buried-under-digital CRM optimizes for send volume. Seat-at-the-table CRM optimizes for the client relationship, which, especially in luxury, is usually the actual asset the brand is trying to protect.

## What I think that seat should look like

CRM should report at the same level as brand and communications, not underneath either of them, because it's the one function with a direct, structured view into how clients actually experience everything the other two are producing. That doesn't mean CRM runs the marketing department. It means CRM's read on the client relationship should be part of what shapes the plan, not just part of how the plan gets executed.

**A question for you:** where does CRM sit in your organization, and honestly, do you think that's the right place for it? I'd like to hear how it looks from where you sit.
